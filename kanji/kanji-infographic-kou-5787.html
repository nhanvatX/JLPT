Learn one Kanji a day with infographic: 攻 (kou)

August 28, 2018   Infographic, Kanji and Vocabulary

[Ảnh: Learn one Kanji a day with infographic: 攻 (kou)]

MEANING:

aggression, attack, criticize, polish

HOW TO READ:

On: kou
Kun: se.meru

JLPT LEVEL: N1

COMPOUNDS:

攻め (seme): attack, offense
攻勢 (kousei): offensive, aggression
攻防 (koubou): offense and defense
侵攻 (shinkou): invasion
専攻 (senkou): major subject, special study
進攻 (shinkou): attack, advance
攻撃 (kougeki): attack, strike, offensive
猛攻 (moukou): fierce attack
速攻 (sokkou): swift attack, quick attack
主攻 (shukou): main attack
反攻 (hankou): counteroffensive
攻略 (kouryaku): capture
攻守 (koushu): offense and defense
攻める (semeru): to attack, to assault
特攻隊 (tokkoutai): suicide squad
正攻法 (seikouhou): frontal attack
総攻撃 (soukougeki): general attack
攻め込む (semekomu): to invade, to attack
テロ攻撃 (terokougeki): terrorist attack

EXAMPLE SENTENCES:

ベルギー侵攻も華々しい緒戦をもって開始された。
berugii shinkou mo hanabanashii shosen o motte kaishi sareta.
The invasion of Belgium also had a sensational opening.

１９６８年のテト攻勢は、落胆を幻滅に変えてしまった。
sen kyuuhyaku rokujuu hachi nen no teto kousei wa, rakutan o genmetsu ni kaete shimatta.
The Tet 1968 Offensive had turned discouragement into disillusion.

批判は真剣に受け止めつつ、個人攻撃と捉えないということを学んだ。
hihan wa shinken ni uketometsutsu, kojin kougeki to toraenai to iu koto o mananda.
I learned to take criticism seriously but not personally.

敵は味方をすり抜けて背後に回り、退路を断ち、両側面から攻めてくる。
teki wa mikata o surinukete haigo ni mawari, tairo o tachi, ryou sokumen kara semete kuru.
The enemy would slip behind you, cut off your avenue of retreat, and then they would hit you on the flanks.

サイゴン政府はすでにデルタ地帯の南半分をゲリラに奪われ、北半分がいま攻防の最中なのである。
saigon seifu wa sude ni deruta chitai no minami hanbun o gerira ni ubaware, kita hanbun ga ima koubou no saichuu na no de aru.
The Saigon government had already forfeited most of the southern half of the Delta to the guerrillas. The northern half was still being contested.

特攻用に数千機保有されていたがたがたの飛行機を除くと、熟練した飛行士もおらず、日本の空軍力は事実上すでに存在していなかった。
tokkouyou ni suusenki hoyuu sarete ita gatagata no hikouki o nozoku to, jukuren shita hikoushi mo orazu, nihon no kuugunryoku wa jijitsujou sude ni sonzai shite inakatta.
Apart from a few thousand rickety planes held in reserve for suicide attacks, Japan’s air force—not only its aircraft, but its skilled pilots as well—had virtually ceased to exist.
